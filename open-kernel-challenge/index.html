Open Kernel Challenge: what actually sped up my kernels

ethduke · 6 November 2026 · about an 8-minute read

TL;DR

  • The biggest jump came from the expert (MoE) kernel. Stock SGLang runs two big matrix multiplies plus about ten small helper launches, and with one token the helpers alone eat 34 of its 80 µs. The fast version is one persistent launch, where each expert starts its second step the moment its own first step ends and its 4-bit weights stream through once at about 4.2 TB/s. With overlapping launches around it, my layer went from 1.67× to 2.23× and finished at 2.30×, rank 115 of 1,299.
  • Attention got the same treatment: one kernel does the whole step, gate included, and splits the cached keys across blocks whose partial results merge through a hardware bulk-reduce, so at 32 tokens it takes 9.3 µs instead of 23.5 µs plus a chain of small steps. Later, an approximate exponential plus FP16 score sums took the attention board from 2.63× to 2.73×.
  • A rule change that allowed approximate math gave prefill its biggest late jump (+5%), but the same idea moved my decoder layer only about +0.36%, because there the GPU mostly waits on memory.

The Open Kernel Challenge asked for faster GPU kernels for Muse Spark Open, an open mixture-of-experts model from Meta. This post is about its kernel track. There, a submission is one Python file that speeds up one part of the model. It runs on a single H200 and gets timed against SGLang's own kernel in the same run.

The serving track is a separate thing: you patch SGLang itself and get scored by throughput on a whole 8×H200 node. So every number here is a kernel speed-up, not serving throughput.

There were five kernel boards. Three cover parts of a decoder layer (attention, MoE and projections), prefill runs the whole layer on long prompts, and the fifth runs the whole layer while generating tokens, which is what sets your overall rank. My first accepted run, on the MoE, read 1.19×.

Honestly, the whole challenge was pretty intense, since you had to keep tweaking something just to hold the same spot on the board.

A slow start

It's pretty obvious you can't win a kernel challenge with SGLang's stock kernels like Marlin, so hand-written CUDA enters the stage. For the first stretch I polished my own kernels. They worked, and they beat SGLang, but my layer was stuck at 1.67×. Then a faster public design turned up, and on the same judge it beat mine on every load. Its expert step does it all in one persistent launch: each expert's second step starts as soon as its own first step ends, and every expert's 4-bit weights stream through once at about 4.2 TB/s. Humbling, but also the most useful thing I learned in the whole challenge.

So I switched. These kernels, with my own changes on top, took my layer to 2.23× and moved me from rank 612 to 127. The MoE went from 1.80× to 2.40×. A day of research on those already tuned kernels then produced 0 surviving ideas out of 9, while two boards sat far behind code I already had. Adopting it took about an hour: projections went from 1.33× to 1.60× (rank 612 to 25), and prefill from 1.52× to 1.80×. Attention later went from 2.00× to 2.63× the same way.

Why the layer got faster

So, what's the layer here? The layer board times everything one GPU does for one decoder layer while the model generates, i.e. the projections, attention, the router, the MoE, and the norms in between. The model stacks 62 of these layers, 46 with sliding-window attention and 16 with full attention, and the score weights them that way. And let's not forget that prefill isn't part of it. That's a separate board running the same layer on long prompts.

So here's the layer at one load, 32 requests making one token each on a sliding-window layer, stock against mine. You can switch between them to see what changed.

Now let's go part by part, in the order the layer runs them.

Projections. These are the plain matrix multiplies: one into attention for the queries, keys, values and gate, one back out, and a pair around the MoE that shrinks the hidden state to the experts' width and grows it back. With only a few tokens per step they're waiting on memory, not math, so the trick is to read less, and the faster code packs the BF16 weights into a lossless 12-bit format with 25% fewer bytes. The projection out of the MoE went from about 22 to 17 µs, though the one after attention actually got a bit slower (5.7 to 6.4 µs).

Attention. In stock SGLang, attention is a whole chain of launches, with the norms, the cache write, combining partial results, and the gate each on their own. The faster version does it all in one launch, splitting each request's cached keys across blocks and letting a hardware bulk-reduce merge the pieces. So you get 9.3 µs instead of 23.5 µs plus all the small steps around it.

Norms and residuals. Between the big steps, the layer adds the residual back in and normalizes. Honestly, these barely moved (about 8 µs in stock, 9 µs in mine), so don't look for the speed-up here.

Router. For each token, the router picks 8 of 256 experts with a small projection, a sigmoid with a bias, and a top-8. Stock runs those as separate small steps, about 13 µs here, while the faster code tucks the router into the MoE's input projection and does the rest in one short 2.2 µs kernel.

MoE. This is the big one, since each token goes through 8 experts on 4-bit weights. Stock runs two big matrix multiplies with sorting, activation and norm helpers in between, while the faster version is one persistent launch: each expert starts its second step as soon as its own first step ends, busy experts get big tiles and quiet ones small tiles, and every expert's weights stream through once at about 4.2 TB/s. It still takes about half the layer, but that's 81.9 µs instead of 125.6 µs plus helpers.

Putting it together. The whole layer ends up as a chain of about nine kernels, and each one lets the next start setting up before it's done, so the gaps between steps mostly disappear. At this load the GPU work fell from 272 to 158 µs, and across all six loads and both layer types, the layer scores 2.30×.

Then measuring became the work

After the switch, the hard part was knowing whether anything really helped. Guessing from profiles often failed. One swap saved 0.5-0.8 µs on its own, then cost 0.7-1.0 µs inside the full layer. What worked was timing old and new code side by side on the same inputs.

Some of the best finds were boring. Settings that one board had already fixed but my layer hadn't picked up gave +0.25%, and a few cheap checks like that added up to +0.61%. The top-ranked research idea actually lost time.

A single score can mislead you in both directions. With the math changes below, that stack was worth about +1% side by side, yet my board row moved only +0.27%. The next change was worth +0.26% to +0.37% side by side, and the row jumped +1.3%, from 2.268× to 2.297×. For anything under about 1%, I trust the side-by-side number, not one score.

A small change with a big boost

The math part is my favorite, because such a small change gave such a visible boost almost instantly. The organizers' Nov 3 update allowed approximate math on the kernel boards, as long as it stays accurate to BF16 precision, and it named attention's exponential and FP16 sums as fair game. Until then I'd stuck to exact math.

Attention turns every score into a weight using an exponential, and it does that for every query and key, so it sits right in the innermost loop. The exact version goes through the GPU's slower math unit, and the result gets rounded to 8 bits right after, so most of that precision was being thrown away. ZhimaoL's approximate exponential gets close enough with a quick bit trick.

In prefill the switch was just a small build change, and it made the attention step about 15-35% faster, with the biggest savings on long contexts. That was worth about +4.4% timed side by side, and it was prefill's biggest late jump, from 1.83× to 1.92× on the board.

It doesn't work everywhere, though. In the decode layer the same kind of change was worth only about +0.36%. There, attention mostly waits on memory loads, and even removing all of its math would add under about 1%. So a small change pays off when it sits in a loop that runs constantly and that step is really waiting on the math. Before speeding up a step, find out what it's waiting for.

One trick worth knowing

One paper I found useful for the research side, if you use agents for this kind of work, is Fork-and-Flush: Escaping Idea Basins in Autoresearch Agents, which I came across through @lxe on X. It shows that separate runs of the same agent can get stuck at very different scores, each circling its own set of ideas. The fix is simple: every so often, fork the agent into parallel runs that keep the workspace but start with a fresh chat, then carry on from the best one. I tried one round on my already tuned layer: three fresh agents, each with 4 test runs and 150 minutes. Two came back with small but real savings of about +0.2% each, and merged they read +0.26% to +0.37% side by side.

What's next

Next up is the serving round, which opens in a couple of days, and I'm looking forward to it. The big difference is the GPU count: the kernel boards time one kernel on a single H200, while serving runs a patched SGLang across a whole node of eight. So it's less about one fast kernel and more about how the full server holds up when eight GPUs share the work.
